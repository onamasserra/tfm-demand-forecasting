\section{1. Introduction}

\begin{frame}{Context}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Demand forecasting is critical for inventory management in the automotive sector
    \item Inaccurate forecasts lead to excess stock (capital tied up) or stockouts (lost sales, production disruptions)
    \item Safety stock acts as a buffer against demand uncertainty
    \item More accurate forecasts = less safety stock needed = lower costs
\end{itemize}
\vfill
\begin{block}{Safety stock formula}
Safety stock is directly proportional to forecast error: $SS = z \times RMSE \times \sqrt{T + L}$
\end{block}
\end{frame}
\note{Demand forecasting is at the heart of inventory management. In the automotive sector, where supply chains are complex and a missing part can halt a production line, getting the forecast right is critical. If you overestimate demand, you tie up capital in excess stock. If you underestimate, you face stockouts and lost sales. Safety stock is the buffer companies keep to protect against this uncertainty, and as you can see in the formula, it is directly proportional to the forecast error. So if we can reduce the forecast error, we directly reduce the safety stock needed, which means lower costs. That is the core idea behind this project.}

\begin{frame}{Objectives}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Conduct a bibliographic review of demand forecasting methods
    \item Explore and prepare a real automotive sector dataset
    \item Implement and compare multiple forecasting models
    \item Evaluate promotional vs non-promotional demand
    \item Compare all models against a naive baseline
    \item Quantify the economic impact on safety stock costs
\end{enumerate}
\end{frame}
\note{The project has six specific objectives. First, a literature review to understand the state of the art. Second, exploring and preparing a real dataset from the automotive sector. Third, implementing and comparing six different forecasting models. Fourth, evaluating how models perform during promotional periods. Fifth, comparing everything against a naive baseline. And sixth, translating the accuracy improvements into actual cost savings for the company.}

That gives us over 629,000 observations. A key characteristic is that 65.3 percent of observations are zero sales, which you can see in the histogram. This is typical for automotive parts where most products do not sell on any given day. All product identifiers are anonymised.}

\section{3. Methodology}

\begin{frame}{Pipeline}
\begin{table}
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{clp{7cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Step} & \textbf{Phase} & \textbf{Description} \\
\midrule
1 & EDA & Exploratory analysis of demand patterns \\
2 & Preprocessing & Stock break imputation (2\%), outlier capping (1.4\%) \\
3 & Feature Engineering & 33 features: calendar, lags, rolling stats, EWMA, promotional \\
4 & Modelling & 6 models trained with rolling validation (3 folds, 30 days) \\
5 & Evaluation & Accuracy metrics, SHAP interpretability, error analysis \\
6 & Economic Impact & Safety stock cost comparison using real company data \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}
\vfill
\begin{itemize}
    \item CRISP-DM methodology
    \item Hyperparameter tuning with Optuna (30 trials per model)
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
\note{The project follows the CRISP-DM methodology in six steps. Starting with exploratory data analysis, then preprocessing where we handled stock breaks and outliers, feature engineering where we created 33 features capturing temporal patterns, modelling with six different algorithms, evaluation using multiple metrics and SHAP interpretability, and finally the economic impact analysis using real company cost data. We used rolling forecast origin validation with three 30-day folds, and Optuna for hyperparameter tuning with 30 trials per model.}

\begin{frame}{Models}
\begin{table}
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
\textbf{Model} & \textbf{Type} \\
\midrule
Naive (lag-7) & Baseline (same weekday last week) \\
ARIMA (Seasonal) & Classical time series (per product) \\
Random Forest & Tree ensemble (bagging) \\
XGBoost & Gradient boosting \\
LightGBM & Gradient boosting (histogram-based) \\
LSTM & Deep learning (recurrent neural network) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}
\vfill
\begin{itemize}
    \item Tree-based models: hyperparameters tuned with Optuna (30 trials each)
    \item ARIMA: auto\_arima with weekly seasonality ($m=7$), fitted to all 861 products
    \item LSTM: single layer (32 units), 14-day input sequences, PyTorch
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
\note{We implemented six models spanning different paradigms. The naive lag-7 baseline simply predicts that today's demand equals last week's same weekday. ARIMA is a classical time series approach fitted individually to each of the 861 products. Then three tree-based ensemble models: Random Forest, XGBoost, and LightGBM, all with hyperparameters tuned by Optuna. And finally LSTM, a deep learning recurrent neural network implemented in PyTorch. This gives us a comprehensive comparison across classical, machine learning, and deep learning approaches.}

\begin{frame}{SHAP Feature Importance}
\begin{columns}
\begin{column}{0.55\textwidth}
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth, height=0.75\textheight, keepaspectratio]{figures/16b_shap_cross_model_comparison.png}

\footnotesize
\begin{itemize}
    \item SHAP computed for all 4 top models
    \item 28-day EWMA dominates across all architectures
    \item Tree models strongly agree ($\rho > 0.86$)
    \item LSTM relies more on calendar features
    \item Core signal is shared: smoothed demand history
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}
\note{We performed SHAP analysis across all four top models, not just LightGBM. The 28-day EWMA is the most important feature for every model. The three tree-based models show strong agreement in feature rankings, with Spearman correlations above 0.86. The LSTM shows moderate correlation with the trees, around 0.4 to 0.5, because it relies more on calendar features like is-weekend and month. This makes sense because the LSTM processes 14-day sequences, so weekend signals accumulate across timesteps. The key takeaway is that regardless of architecture, smoothed recent demand history is the strongest predictor.}

\section{6. Economic Impact}

\begin{frame}{Safety Stock Cost Analysis}
\begin{block}{Methodology}
$SS_i = z \times RMSE_i \times \sqrt{T_i + L_i}$ \quad\quad $DailyCost_i = 5\% \times price_i \times SS_i$
\end{block}
\vfill
\begin{table}
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Model} & \textbf{Total SS} & \textbf{Annual Cost} & \textbf{Savings} \\
\midrule
Naive (lag-7) & 12,544 units & \euro11,398,048 & -- \\
Random Forest & 9,105 units & \euro8,310,660 & \euro3,087,388 (27.1\%) \\
XGBoost & 9,090 units & \euro8,307,074 & \euro3,090,974 (27.1\%) \\
LightGBM & 9,133 units & \euro8,341,910 & \euro3,056,137 (26.8\%) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}
\vfill
All three tree-based models save approximately \textbf{\euro3.1 million per year}.
\end{frame}
\note{Now the economic impact, which is the ultimate goal of this project. Using the safety stock formula with a 95 percent service level and real per-product cost parameters provided by the company, we computed the annual safety stock cost for each model. The naive baseline costs 11.4 million euros per year. All three tree-based models reduce this to approximately 8.3 million, saving around 3.1 million euros annually. That is a 27 percent reduction in safety stock costs, achieved purely through better forecasting.}

\section{7. Conclusions}

\begin{frame}{Key Findings}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Tree-based ML models reduce forecast error by \textbf{28\%} vs naive baseline
    \item The three tree models (RF, XGBoost, LightGBM) are nearly interchangeable
    \item Feature engineering (EWMA, rolling stats, lags) may matter more than model choice for this data
    \item Forecast improvement translates to \textbf{\euro3.1M annual savings} in safety stock costs
    \item Results are robust across service levels and product segments
\end{enumerate}
\end{frame}
\note{To summarise the key findings: tree-based ML models reduce forecast error by 28 percent compared to the naive baseline. The three tree models are nearly interchangeable in accuracy. The similarity in their performance suggests that for this dataset, the choice of features may be more influential than the choice of algorithm. Most importantly, the forecast improvement translates directly into 3.1 million euros of annual savings in safety stock costs. And these results are robust across different service levels and demand segments.}

\begin{frame}{Limitations and Future Work}
\textbf{Limitations:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Single company, single sector (automotive)
    \item One-day-ahead forecast horizon
    \item No external variables (macroeconomic, competitor data)
\end{itemize}
\vfill
\textbf{Future work:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Multi-step forecasting over the full protection period
    \item Probabilistic forecasting (prediction intervals)
    \item Automated deployment pipeline for daily operational use
    \item Product-specific forecasting strategies
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
\note{The main limitations are that this is a single-company, single-sector study, the models predict one day ahead using actual past values, and no external variables like macroeconomic data were included. For future work, multi-step forecasting over the full protection period would be valuable, as would probabilistic forecasting to generate prediction intervals rather than point estimates. An automated deployment pipeline for daily operational use and product-specific forecasting strategies are also natural next steps.}
